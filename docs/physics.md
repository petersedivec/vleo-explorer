# Physics Specification

The source of truth for every equation in VLEO Trade Study Explorer. The Python reference
model (`reference/`) and the TypeScript model (`web/src/model/`) both implement this file.
When code and this file disagree, one of them is a bug.

Status per section: **[v1]** implemented in v1 · **[v2]** / **[v3]** later · **[draft]**
equations set, defaults to be refined when the feature is built.

## 0. Conventions

- SI units internally. Names carry unit suffixes in code: `altitude_m`, `thrust_N`, `power_W`.
- Circular orbits only (v1). Altitude *h* is above the equatorial radius.

  Every teaching default is editable in the app.

## 1. Orbit [v1]

Radius r = R⊕ + h.

| Quantity | Equation |
|---|---|
| Circular speed | v = √(μ / r) |
| Period | T = 2π √(r³ / μ) |
| Orbits per day | day / T |
| Ground speed (sub-satellite, ignoring Earth rotation) | v_g = v · R⊕ / r |
| Nodal regression (J₂) | Ω̇ = −(3/2) J₂ (R⊕/r)² n cos i, with n = 2π / T |
| Sun-synchronous inclination | i_SSO = acos(−Ω̇_sun / ((3/2) J₂ (R⊕/r)² n)), Ω̇_sun = 2π / 365.2422 days |

Check: h = 250 km → T ≈ 89.6 min → ≈ 16.1 orbits/day.

## 2. Atmosphere [v1]

### 2.1 Orbit-averaged NRLMSIS 2.1 tables (primary model)

Generated by `reference/build_atmos.py` using `pymsis` (version pinned in
`reference/requirements.txt`). Output: `web/src/data/atmosphere.json`.

**Grid**
- Altitude: 100–1,000 km, 5 km steps. Interpolate **log₁₀ density linearly** in altitude.
- F10.7 (daily = 81-day average): 70, 100, 150, 200, 250 sfu. Interpolate log density linearly in F10.7.
- Ap (constant over the day): 4, 15, 50, 150. Interpolate log density linearly in Ap.
- Inclination: 0°, 30°, 45°, 53°, 70°, 97.5°. Interpolate linearly in inclination.
- Fields: total mass density ρ (kg/m³) and atomic-oxygen number density n_O (m⁻³).

**Orbit averaging** (for each grid cell)
- Latitude: sample the argument of latitude u uniformly on [0, π/2] (18 samples, symmetric
  by hemisphere); latitude φ = asin(sin i · sin u). This weights latitudes by time spent there.
- Local solar time: 8 longitudes, 45° apart, at fixed UT.
- Season: 4 dates (equinoxes and solstices).
- ρ is the arithmetic mean over all samples (drag is linear in ρ).

**Assumption to state in the app:** averaging over local time ignores that a
sun-synchronous orbit stays at one local time. A dawn-dusk orbit sees somewhat lower
density than the average; a noon orbit somewhat higher.

### 2.2 Solar-activity modes

| Mode | F10.7 | Ap |
|---|---|---|
| Low (solar minimum) | 70 | 4 |
| Moderate | 150 | 15 |
| High (solar maximum) | 250 | 15 |

- **Exploration mode** uses one of the three rows.
- **Mission-life mode** [v2] uses a time series F10.7(t) from the published cycle history and

## 3. Drag [v1]

| Quantity | Equation |
|---|---|
| Drag force | D = ½ ρ v² C_D A_ref |
| Ballistic coefficient | B = m / (C_D A_ref) |
| Drag acceleration | a_D = D / m = ½ ρ v² / B |

- v is the inertial speed; atmospheric co-rotation (a few-percent effect) is neglected in v1.
- **C_D presets (teaching defaults):** compact body 2.2; long slender body 2.6; flat plate
  normal to flow 2.4. C_D in free-molecular flow depends on surface material and gas-surface
  accommodation; ranges come from ADBSat-type panel-method results.
- **Edge-on panels still have drag.** A flat panel parallel to the flow (an edge-on solar
  array, a nadir-facing antenna) exchanges momentum with molecules hitting it at grazing
  incidence. Equivalent drag area ≈ k_graze × panel area, with **k_graze = 0.07** (teaching
  default; range 0.03–0.1, from free-molecular flat-plate theory with diffuse reflection at
  orbital speed ratios s ≈ 7–8).
- **Sun-tracking arrays** rotate once per orbit; their orbit-averaged projected area in the
  ram direction is ≈ (2/π) × array area (k_track = 0.64), plus the grazing term.

Reference area: A_ref = A_body + k_array · A_array + A_payload, where A_payload is ram-facing area a payload
adds outside the body, e.g. an edge-on antenna's edge plus k_graze × its face (§9).

## 4. Decay without thrust [v1]

From Gauss's variational equations for a circular orbit with tangential drag:

  da/dt = −√(μ a) · ρ(a) / B

- With density a function of altitude only (exploration mode), integrate in altitude:
  t = ∫ B / (√(μ a) ρ(a)) da from reentry to the start altitude. This is exact and stable.
  Python: `solve_ivp` over a; TypeScript: composite Simpson on 0.25 km steps (`decayLifetime_s`),
  and for interactive sweeps a cached cumulative trapezoid table I(h) on 0.25 km steps so that
  lifetime = B · I(h) (`decayIntegralTable`; agrees with Simpson within 0.1%).
- Mission-life mode (time-varying F10.7) integrates in time [v2].
- **Reentry altitude** h_re = 120 km (below this, reentry follows within hours).
- **Lifetime** = time from h₀ to h_re.
- **FCC 5-year rule check:** disposal time from end-of-mission altitude ≤ 5 years.

**Verification (analytic):** for a single exponential band with constant √(μa) ≈ √(μ r₀),
  t(h₀ → h₁) = (B H / (√(μ r₀) ρ(h₀))) · (1 − exp(−(h₀ − h₁)/H)).
The integrator must match this within 1%.

## 5. Propulsion [v1]

### 5.1 Electric propulsion

| Quantity | Equation |
|---|---|
| Thruster input power | P = T g₀ Isp / (2η) |
| Thrust-to-power (derived, not an input) | T/P = 2η / (g₀ Isp) |
| Mass flow | ṁ = T / (g₀ Isp) |

η is the total **system** efficiency (jet power ÷ electrical input power, including power-processing-unit
losses). Laboratory papers usually quote thruster-only efficiency, which is higher. Isp and η are inputs; thrust-to-power
is derived so the three cannot disagree.

### 5.2 Cold gas and monopropellant

Thrust is not power-limited. Power = fixed heater/valve power P_fixed (teaching default
10 W for cold gas, 20 W for monoprop), independent of thrust.

### 5.3 Technology presets (generic classes, ~50 kg spacecraft, 20–200 W)

Small thrusters are much less efficient than kilowatt-class ones, so Isp and η are set to published
small-satellite values (derived η = T·g₀·Isp / 2P where only thrust, power and Isp are published).

| Class | Isp (s) | η | Thrust per unit | Thrust-to-power | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold gas | 65 | — | 10 mN | — | N₂ 65–71 s; butane 50–69 s |
| Monopropellant (green) | 220 | — | 1 N | — | AF-M315E ≥ 220 s |
| Electrospray (ionic liquid) | 1,500 | 0.35 | 0.15 mN per module | ≈ 48 µN/W | Isp 150–4,000 s by mode (droplet/colloid ≈ 150–250 s; small ion-mode chips ≈ 600–1,500 s after losses; pure-ion beams ≈ 3,000–4,000 s), so 1,500 s is at the top end for small modules; thruster-only η ≈ 0.3–0.65 × PPU 0.66–0.9 → system ≈ 0.25–0.55, ≈ 30–90 µN/W (derived, not published; the high end is optimistic because high η comes with high Isp); single heads ≈ 5–100 µN, so a module is several heads |

(SSC22-X-08); GomSpace NanoProp. Most are manufacturer figures. Efficiency that varies with operating
power within a technology is not modelled.

### 5.4 Drag makeup at constant altitude

- Required average thrust: T_req = D. With thrusting duty cycle d (fraction of the orbit the
  thruster can fire), required thrust while firing: T_fire = D / d.
- Propellant flow: ṁ = D / (g₀ Isp) — **independent of spacecraft mass.**
- **Propellant life:** t_prop = m_prop g₀ Isp / D.
- **Total-impulse life:** t_I = I_max / D, where I_max is the thruster's rated total impulse.
- **Δv per year:** Δv_yr = (D / m) · yr.

### 5.5 Mass history [v1]

dm/dt = −T / (g₀ Isp). Mass is time-stepped together with altitude. Mass matters for:
- descent and orbit raising (acceleration = T / m);
- decay after a thruster failure (lighter spacecraft → lower B → faster decay);
- Δv budgets: Δv = g₀ Isp ln(m₀ / m_f).

### 5.6 Mission life

t_life = min(t_prop, t_I, t_design), with t_design a design-life input (teaching default 5 yr).

### 5.7 Propulsion trade [v1.3]

A purely technical comparison of technologies on the same spacecraft (payload, bus, arrays, storm
margin and solar activity from Explore). For each technology the closed sizing loop (§7) sizes thruster
power, arrays and dry mass using that technology's preset Isp, efficiency and fixed system mass.
Propellant is then added in one of two ways:

- **Equal requirement** (hold altitude h for life L): m_p = D L / (g₀ Isp); tank = f_tank m_p;
  **wet mass = m_dry + m_p (1 + f_tank)**. The lightest feasible technology wins.
- **Equal total mass** (wet-mass budget M): m_p = (M − m_dry) / (1 + f_tank);
  **life = m_p g₀ Isp / D**. The longest life wins.

Comparing at equal *propellant* mass is misleading: it ignores that high Isp needs more power, larger
arrays (more mass and drag) and different tankage.

**Optimal-Isp curve:** a generic electric thruster with fixed efficiency η and tank fraction, swept over
Isp. Low Isp → much propellant; high Isp → much power, array and drag. The minimum of wet mass vs Isp
is the optimal Isp for the mission; it moves with altitude and life.

**Winner map:** the equal-requirement winner over a grid of altitude × life. The loop is run once per
technology per altitude (it does not depend on life); propellant is added per life.

Estimates (editable in `web/src/model/propulsion.ts`), reviewed by the project author and checked
against public data, Nov 2026:

| Class | Tank fraction f_tank | Fixed system mass (kg) | Minimum power (W) | Wear life per unit (N·s) | Each extra unit (kg) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold gas | 0.6 | 0.5 | — | not limiting | — |
| Monopropellant | 0.15 | 1.5 | — | not limiting | — |
| Electrospray | 0.08 | 0.5 | 2 | 300 (band 50–2,000) | 0.2 |
| FEEP | 0.10 | 0.8 | 10 | 5,000 | 0.9 |
| Gridded ion | 0.15 | 0.8 | 35 | 5,500 | 1.0 |
| Hall (xenon) | 0.15 | 2.5 | 75 | 50,000 | 0.7 |
| Hall (krypton) | 0.30 | 2.5 | 100 | 40,000 | 0.7 |
| RF plasma | 0.15 | 4 | 50 | not limiting (no electrodes) | — |

Basis: xenon tanks 0.07–0.09 for large tanks, higher for small; krypton stores at about one third of
xenon's density (0.25–0.45); electrospray wear life is the least certain value (see below);
FEEP ~5 kN·s and small ion ~5.5 kN·s per unit; small Hall ~50 kN·s (krypton erodes faster: estimate);
RF plasma uses flight-unit values rather than lab-scale ones: flown units range from about 50 W (an
iodine plasma thruster, 2021) to the 300–500 W class (≈ 7 kg dry).

**Electrospray wear life (reviewed and fact-checked Nov 2026).** 300 N·s per 0.15 mN module is ≈ 560 h at rated
thrust. No public test reports N·s per module directly; the band below is scaled from test hours to a 0.15 mN
module. Ion- and mixed-mode tests: passively fed ion chips emitted ≈ 90–172 h without current decay (≈ 50–90 N·s
scaled; Krejci et al., J. Spacecraft & Rockets 54(2), 2017); a JPL beam-target test-method run > 470 h (≈ 250 N·s
scaled, a lower bound; JPL poster R23119, 2024); a 2020 lifetime test of one commercial module (AIAA 2020-3614),
≈ 92 N·s. Droplet-mode (colloid) heritage: ST7 on LISA Pathfinder ran ≈ 2,400 h in flight, and its ground life
test ran ≈ 3,400–3,500 h and delivered ≈ 245 N·s per head against a 140 N·s requirement (NTRS 20170010216;
Anderson et al., Phys. Rev. D 98, 102005, 2018, which also notes the flight anomalies would not be acceptable for
a primary propulsion system). Several of these tests stopped before wear-out, so they are lower bounds; hours do
not carry over directly between droplet and ion modes, and modelling shows small grid-geometry changes alter
predicted life up to several-fold while misalignment cuts it by 20–50% (Thuppul, Wright, Collins, Ziemer & Wirz,
Aerospace 7(8):108, 2020). The app therefore uses **300 N·s** as a central teaching estimate — slightly above the
longest published ion-mode run, justified only because those runs stopped before wear-out — and shows a
**50–2,000 N·s band** (≈ 90–3,700 h); the top of the band rests on droplet-mode heritage only. The Propulsion
trade what-if explores the band. This number moves the electrospray results more than any other (in the review's
sensitivity runs, ≈ 0 → ≈ 90 clear CubeSat wins out of 756 winner-map cells going from 100 to 300 N·s), so treat
electrospray wins as conditional on it.

Other electrospray values are teaching estimates: extra-module mass 0.2 kg (plausible 0.1–0.3 kg, the second
largest lever), minimum power 2 W (plausible 1–5 W, set by power electronics rather than the emitters), fixed
system mass 0.5 kg. The generic PPU mass of 0.02 kg/W (§7) is conservative for electrospray; a published 10 W
electrospray PPU is ≈ 0.004 kg/W (39 g at 10 W; Blázquez-Plaza et al., CEAS Space J. 17, 2024). Flight systems with control electronics, heaters and a
neutraliser can fall far below laboratory thrust per watt (ST7: a few µN/W at system level, ≈ 17 W per
four-head cluster at up to 30 µN per head).

**Thruster wear (simple model).** Units carried n = max(1, ⌈T_sized / T_unit⌉, ⌈D · L / I_unit⌉),
where L is the life the thrusters must survive (design life in Explore and Constellation; the required
life in the Propulsion trade). Each unit beyond the first adds its mass inside the sizing loop. Total
impulse capacity n · I_unit caps mission life. For equal-mass comparisons, the longest life L with units
sized for L is found by bisection. An "ignore wear" switch removes the limit as a sensitivity check.

Plus PPU mass α_ppu P for electric thrusters (§7).

**Minimum operating power** (table above). Thruster power is max(required, minimum); the power system is sized for
it and the thruster fires intermittently, so propellant use still follows drag.

**Ties and sensitivity:** a winner within 10% of the runner-up's wet mass is shown as a tie (faded).
An "equal fixed masses" switch gives every technology the same 2 kg fixed system mass, to show how much
of each result comes from the estimates; the generic optimal-Isp curve always uses 2 kg.

**Not yet modelled:** efficiency that varies with operating power within a technology, and battery mass.

## 6. Eclipse and power [v1]

### 6.1 Eclipse

β = angle between the orbit plane and the Sun vector. With sin β* = R⊕ / r:
- if |β| ≥ β*: no eclipse;
- else eclipse fraction f_e = (1/π) · acos( √(h² + 2 R⊕ h) / (r cos β) ).

β* ≈ 73° at 300 km, ≈ 78° at 150 km. A dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit has β ranging
roughly 60°–90° over the year, so **it has eclipse seasons in VLEO.** β is a user input; the
default β = 0 is the worst case. (Automatic seasonal β for sun-synchronous orbits is not yet
implemented.)

### 6.2 Solar array sizing (after SMAD)

  P_array = (P_e T_e / X_e + P_d T_d / X_d) / T_d

where T_e, T_d are eclipse and daylight durations, P_e, P_d the loads in each, and X_e = 0.6,
X_d = 0.8 (direct energy transfer). Array area:

  A_array = P_array / (S · η_cell · F_pack · cos θ_avg · L_d)

Teaching defaults: η_cell = 0.30, F_pack = 0.85, L_d (life degradation) = 0.85;
cos θ_avg = 1 for sun-tracking, 0.64 for body-fixed/edge-on arrays. Array areal mass 3 kg/m².

### 6.3 Thruster duty cycle

If the thruster fires in daylight only (no battery support), d = 1 − f_e. Otherwise d = 1 and
eclipse thrusting is charged to the battery through P_e.

## 7. Closed sizing loop [v1]

Unknowns: dry mass m_dry, array area A_array, thruster power P_thr. Iterate:

1. Loads: P_d = P_bus + P_payload + P_thr; P_e as configured (§6.3).
2. A_array from §6.2.
3. Body frontal area: A_body = s_f · (m_dry / ρ_bulk)^(2/3); ρ_bulk = 400 kg/m³, shape factor
   s_f = 0.6 (teaching defaults), or a fixed user value; never less than the payload's own ram-facing area
   (e.g. a telescope barrel): A_body = max(that, A_payload).
4. A_ref and D from §3, using ρ × k_storm for sizing.
5. P_thr from §5.1 with T_fire = D / d.
6. m_dry = m_payload + m_bus_fixed + f_struct · m_dry + σ_array A_array + α_ppu P_thr + m_thruster_fixed
   (solved for m_dry each pass). Teaching defaults: f_struct = 0.25, α_ppu = 0.02 kg/W.
7. Repeat until relative change < 10⁻⁷. The update is monotone (more mass → more area → more drag →
   more power → more mass), so plain fixed-point iteration from the drag-free design rises steadily
   to the solution, at a rate set by the effective loop gain (up to 20,000 passes near the wall).

**Infeasible** (no design closes) if:
- the loop diverges — effective loop gain ≥ 1, so mass grows without bound (detected as m_dry > 10⁶ kg); or
- it converges, but to a dry mass more than **10×** the same spacecraft with no drag to fight
  (`MASS_GROWTH_LIMIT`, a stated practicality limit, not physics).

The drag-free reference sizes arrays for bus and payload loads only.

**Two independent solutions.** The TypeScript model solves the loop by fixed-point iteration as above. The
Python reference (`reference/vleo_model.py`, `size_design`) solves it differently: for a trial dry mass, steps
2–5 are linear in A_array (above the minimum power), so A_array = (L E_b + c A_body E_t) / (p (1 − G)), with
c the thruster watts per square metre of storm-drag area, E_b and E_t the array watts per watt of all-orbit and
thruster load (§6.2), p the array watts per m² and G the loop gain of §7.1; the dry mass is then the first root
of the mass balance above the drag-free mass, found by scanning and bisection (Brent). Golden vectors from the
Python solution (`reference/golden/physics.json`, `sizing` and `as_built`) are checked by
`web/src/model/golden.test.ts` to 10⁻⁵.

### 7.1 Loop gain (why there is a hard floor)

Ignoring the mass feedback, each extra watt of thruster power needs extra array area, which
adds drag, which needs more thruster power. The loop gain is

  G = (½ ρ v² C_D k_array) · (g₀ Isp / (2η d)) · (1 / p_array)

where ρ is the **storm-margin density** ρ·k_storm used for sizing, and p_array is the thruster power one
square metre of array can support:
p_array = S η_cell F_pack L_d cos θ_avg / E, with E = 1/X_d + (f_e / ((1 − f_e) X_e) if the
thruster also fires in eclipse). Mass feedback (array and PPU mass enlarge the body) adds a little
more gain, so the effective gain is slightly above G: with defaults the wall falls where G ≈ 0.9–1.
A test checks that designs with G < 0.85 close and designs with G > 1.05 do not. **If G ≥ 1 no design exists at
that altitude.** Because ρ grows exponentially as h drops, G crosses 1 at a critical altitude
— the hard left wall of the U-curve. Higher Isp raises G: high-Isp thrusters hit the floor at
higher altitude unless arrays are edge-on (small k_array).

## 7a. As-built mode [v1.2]

For a spacecraft that already exists, the sizing loop is skipped. Inputs: dry mass m_dry, effective drag
area A (including arrays), C_D, maximum continuous thrust T_max, Isp and propellant.

| Quantity | Equation |
|---|---|
| Thrust margin | M = T_max · d / D (nominal density) |
| Holds altitude | M ≥ 1 |
| Thruster power to hold altitude (electric) | P = (D / d) · g₀ Isp / (2η) |
| Decay rate with no thrust | ȧ = √(μ a) · ρ / B, with B at launch (wet) mass |

Mission life, propellant life, natural lifetime and disposal follow §4–§5 with the given mass and area.

**Heritage checks** (`web/src/data/heritage.ts`) compare an observed outcome with the model evaluated at
the ends of the F10.7 range the mission experienced, with no storm margin and no design-life cap. A
result within 10% of the model range counts as "within".

## 8. Descent from a rideshare orbit [v1]

- Edelbaum low-thrust Δv between circular orbits (coplanar): Δv = |v₁ − v₂|.
  Check: 500 → 240 km ≈ 148 m/s.
- Time: da/dt = −2 √(a³/μ) · (T/m) − √(μ a) ρ / B with dm/dt = −T/(g₀ Isp) (§5.5). Integrate
  in a with state (t, m), since da/dt < 0 throughout: dt/da = 1/ȧ, dm/da = ṁ/ȧ. Python:
  `solve_ivp`; TypeScript: RK4 on 0.25 km steps. Drag assists the descent.

## 9. Missions: requirement-based payloads [v1.5]

Each mission states a requirement on the ground; the payload that meets it is sized at every altitude
(Constellation tab, Design mode). Code: `web/src/model/missions.ts` (parameters in `MISSION_PARAMS`);
Python reference: `reference/vleo_model.py` (§9 functions), checked against each other by golden vectors.
All reference values below are **teaching estimates** with the reasoning given; none describes a product.

### 9.1 Optical Earth observation

- Pixels matched to the diffraction limit, so the ground sample distance at nadir is GSD ≈ Q λ h / D, Q = 1.22
  (Rayleigh convention), λ = 550 nm. (GSD itself is pixel pitch × h / focal length; this sets the focal length
  to match. The formula is somewhat conservative against flown high-resolution imagers.) Required aperture: **D = Q λ h / GSD** (∝ h). Default GSD 0.8 m → D ≈ 0.25 m at 300 km.
- Mass and power scale from a reference instrument (D_ref = 0.5 m, m_ref = 100 kg, P_ref = 100 W):
  m = m_ref (D / D_ref)^2.5, P = P_ref (D / D_ref)². Exponents are the app's own estimates (the New SMAD's
  scaling rule uses the aperture ratio cubed for both): mass would grow as D³ at fixed focal ratio and density,
  lightweighted optics pull it down to ≈ 2–3; at fixed GSD the detector does not change, so power growth is
  mainly thermal control of larger optics, taken as ≈ D².
- Imaging swath = 12,000 pixels across × GSD (9.6 km at 0.8 m); imaging duty 2% of the orbit (small imagers
  are limited by downlink and power). Used only for cost per km² imaged (§11).
- Telescope barrel (compact, folded design): diameter 1.3 D, length 1.5 D. Pointed at nadir, its **side**
  faces the flow, so the body's frontal area is at least 1.3 D × 1.5 D (§7 step 3).
- Not modelled: detector-limited (rather than diffraction-limited) designs, off-nadir resolution loss,
  minimum solar elevation (dawn-dusk orbits are poor for optical imaging).

### 9.2 Synthetic aperture radar (stripmap)

- Slant range from the incidence angle θ at the ground: R = −R⊕ cos θ + √(R⊕² cos² θ + 2 R⊕ h + h²).
  Earth curvature makes the look angle smaller than θ, so h < R < h / cos θ.
- **Ambiguity floor on antenna area:** A_min = 4 v λ R tan θ / c. Antenna A = 1.2 A_min (margin), length ÷
  height = 8. X band (9.65 GHz, λ ≈ 3.1 cm), θ = 30°. Check: ≈ 1.0 m² at 500 km (A ≈ 1.25 m², 3.2 m × 0.4 m),
  the small end of flown small radar satellites (≈ 1.3–8 m²). The floor binds only when a design wants both the
  best azimuth resolution and the full beam-limited swath; designs that give up some of either can use smaller
  antennas (Freeman et al., IEEE TGRS 38(1), 2000).
- **Sensitivity:** NESZ ∝ R³ λ v / (P_avg A²). Imaging power scaled from a reference design:
  P_img = P_ref (R/R_ref)³ (v/v_ref) (A_ref/A)² · 10^((NESZ_ref − NESZ)/10), with P_ref = 1,000 W (DC while
  imaging) at h_ref = 500 km, A_ref = 1.2 A_min(h_ref), NESZ_ref = −17 dB at ≈ 3 m ground-range resolution
  (NESZ ∝ 1 / resolution; a radar-equation check with ≈ 300 W average RF and typical losses gives ≈ −17 dB). With A ∝ v R, **P_img ∝ R / v**,
  roughly ∝ R: radar gains far less from low altitude than optics.
- Orbit-average payload power = 20 W standby + 5% imaging duty (≈ 5 min per orbit) × P_img. Mass = 15 kg electronics + 15 kg/m² × A.
- Stripmap swath ≈ λ R / (H cos θ), H = antenna height (elevation beam spread over the slant range, projected on
  the ground): ≈ 52 km at 500 km. Used only for cost per km² imaged (§11).
- Drag: long axis along track, face looking sideways, so the flow sees the antenna's edge (height × 0.1 m)
  plus grazing drag k_graze A. This is extra ram-facing area outside the body (`payloadDragArea_m2`, §3).
- Not modelled: battery for imaging bursts, resolution and swath trades, ScanSAR/spotlight modes.

### 9.3 Direct-to-device communications

- Slant range at minimum user elevation ε: d = √(r² − R⊕² cos² ε) − R⊕ sin ε (ε shared with §10.3).
- **Uplink:** C/N₀ = EIRP_phone + G_sat − L_fs − L_other − 10 log₁₀ T_sys − 10 log₁₀ k_B (dB),
  L_fs = 20 log₁₀(4π d / λ) → required G_sat → A_link = G λ² / (4π η_ant). Defaults follow 3GPP TR 38.821
  §6.1 (handheld 23 dBm, 0 dBi → −7 dBW); T_sys = 500 K is an estimate (the 3GPP S-band set implies ≈ 800 K,
  which does not change the result because the cell size dominates by ≈ 60×).
- **Cell size:** a beam about λ/L wide covers a cell of width w at range d, so L ≈ λ d / w → A_beam = L².
- Antenna area A = max(A_link, A_beam), both ∝ d². Defaults: f = 2 GHz, phone EIRP −7 dBW,
  T_sys = 500 K, required C/N₀ = 50 dB-Hz, L_other = 3 dB, η_ant = 0.6, w = 50 km. At these values the
  cell size, not the uplink, sets the antenna (≈ 3.8 m² at 300 km, 25° elevation).
- Payload power: per beam, delivering the same power flux to the ground needs transmit power ∝ d² / G, and
  G ∝ A ∝ d², so the two cancel; but a higher satellite sees more cells. Power is therefore scaled with
  footprint area to keep the same power per cell: P = 100 W × (1 − cos λ) / (1 − cos λ_ref), λ_ref at 300 km
  and 25° (§10.1). Mass = 20 kg electronics + 10 kg/m² × A.
- Drag: faces nadir, so edge-on: √A × 0.1 m edge plus k_graze A, outside the body.
- Not modelled: capacity per beam, spectrum sharing, downlink link margin and latency (which favour lower
  orbits), and edge-of-coverage cells being stretched by ≈ 1/sin ε.

### 9.4 How the payload enters the satellite

Payload mass and power replace the Explore payload in the sizing loop (§7). The telescope barrel sets a
minimum body frontal area; antennas add `payloadDragArea_m2` to the reference area. As-built spacecraft
are not resized. "Scale Explore payload" keeps the simpler (h / 300 km)^k rule (§10.4) for comparison.

## 10. Constellation sizing [draft — analytic in v1, simulated in v2]

### 10.1 Geometry

- Earth central angle for off-nadir angle η: elevation ε = acos(sin η · r / R⊕);
  λ = 90° − η − ε.
- Earth central angle for minimum elevation ε: λ = acos(R⊕ cos ε / r) − ε.
- Swath width: W = 2 R⊕ λ.

### 10.2 Imaging revisit (swath-limited)

Over a revisit time t_r, N satellites provide N · (t_r / T) · 2 equator crossings (ascending
and descending, if both are usable — 1 for optical in SSO), each covering W / sin i_eff along
the equator. Satellites needed at the equator:

  N_revisit = ceil( 2π R⊕ · sin i / (n_pass · (t_r / T) · W) )

with n_pass = 2 crossings per orbit (1 for optical in sun-synchronous orbit, daylight passes
only). A swath crosses the equator at angle i, so it spans W / sin i along the equator.

Accounts for Earth rotation only through the uniform-spacing assumption; refined by
simulation in v2. Scales ∝ 1/W ∝ ~1/h.

### 10.3 Continuous coverage (comms)

Global or latitude-band single coverage, hexagonal packing estimate:

  N_cont ≈ (4π · sin φ_max) / ((3√3/2) λ²)

for coverage up to latitude φ_max (λ in radians, small-angle). This ideal packing is a lower bound;
real Walker / streets-of-coverage designs need somewhat more. Refined with Rider's method and by
simulation in v2. Scales ∝ ~1/λ² ∝ ~1/h².

### 10.4 Sustainment and the optimum altitude [v1.4]

- **Payload growth with altitude:** to keep the same ground performance, payload mass and power scale
  as (h / h_ref)^k, h_ref = 300 km (where the Explore payload is defined). Teaching defaults: k = 2.5
  for optical imaging at fixed resolution (aperture ∝ h, mass ∝ aperture^~2.5); k = 2 for a
  direct-to-device antenna at fixed link margin (area ∝ h²); k = 0 keeps the payload fixed. Design mode
  only. The default is now requirement-based payload sizing (§9); this scaling remains as an option.
- At each altitude the satellite is sized by the closed loop (§7) with the scaled payload. In Design
  mode it carries exactly the propellant needed to hold altitude for the design life,
  m_p = D · t_design / (g₀ Isp), plus tank (§5.7), so its life is min(design life, total-impulse life).
  (Otherwise the optimum would be set by whatever propellant load was chosen in Explore.) As built, the
  spacecraft and its propellant are fixed.
- **Replacement rate** = N / t_life. **Mass to orbit per year** = replacement rate × wet mass
  (steady state; excludes launch-vehicle adapters and dispensers). **Deployment mass** = N × wet mass.
- **Optimum altitude** = minimum of mass to orbit per year over feasible altitudes. Because curves can be
  flat, the app also shows the band of altitudes within 10% of that minimum (`nearOptimumBand`). Left wall: drag
  (short lives, larger power systems, no closure). Right wall: payload growth. For continuous coverage
  with k = 2, footprint area (∝ ~h²) cancels payload growth and the optimum moves to the top of the range.

## 11. Cost [v1.5]

Code: `web/src/model/cost.ts`; shown on the Constellation tab. Every parameter is a low–high range of
**teaching assumptions** in US$ of about 2025 (not market data); results are bands rounded to two significant
figures, with the breakdown always visible. Steady-state replacement view. Not included: development
(non-recurring) cost, financing and the time value of money, insurance, launch failures, spectrum and
licensing, large-scale data processing and archiving, company overheads and profit. Fact-checked
independently (Nov 2026; sources seen in search snippets only).

| Quantity | Equation |
|---|---|
| First-unit cost | C₁ = c_bus · (m_dry − m_payload) + c_payload · m_payload |
| Learning curve (Wright, cumulative average) | average cost per unit over N units = C₁ · N^(log₂ L): each doubling of N multiplies the average by L |
| Learning rate L | 95% below 10 units, 90% for 10–49, 85% for 50 or more (SMAD 3rd ed. §20, after Meisl & Morales, Aerospace Corp.); applied by segment (95% to 10, 90% from 10 to 50, 85% beyond) so cost is continuous in N |
| Launch per satellite | c_launch · m_wet (to the release orbit; descent to VLEO is modelled in §8, not costed) |
| Replacements per year | N / t_life (§10.4) |
| **Cost per year to sustain** | C_avg · N / t_life + launch · N / t_life + c_ops · N + C_fixed |
| Deployment | N · (C_avg + launch) |

Replacements are later, cheaper units on the curve; averaging over the first N is conservative.

| Assumption (US$, ~2025) | Low | High | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| c_bus, first unit, per kg of bus | 30k | 100k | Small-satellite buses span tens of thousands of dollars per kg in production to over 100k/kg for one-off government builds; older Small Satellite Cost Model examples (≈ $3.8M for a 60 kg microsatellite, FY95) fall inside. Mass-produced broadband satellites sit below this band |
| c_payload, first unit, per kg | 60k | 250k | Payloads cost more per kg than buses (optics, radar electronics, phased arrays) |
| c_launch, per kg of wet mass | 6k | 30k | Low: published rideshare list prices to sun-synchronous orbit (≈ $6–7k/kg, 2024–26, 50 kg minimum). High: a full dedicated small launcher (≈ $25k/kg), more if partly full |
| c_ops, per satellite per year | 50k | 400k | Automated fleet operations at the low end; ground-station-as-a-service antenna time alone can reach ≈ $0.2–0.5M per satellite per year |
| C_fixed, per year | 1M | 15M | Ground segment, mission control and some data handling; large-scale processing and archiving would add more |

**Cost per unit of service** (a cost to collect or serve, not a market price):
- Imaging (optical, radar): dollars per km² the sensors could collect. Area per year = N × swath × ground
  speed × imaging duty × year (swaths and duties in §9.1–9.2). An **upper bound**: downlink capacity is not
  modelled. Commercial imagery sells for thousands of times more per km² (≈ $16–60/km² for 30–50 cm
  products) because most collected area is never sold (cloud, duplicates, untasked areas) and prices carry
  everything excluded above.
- Phone service: dollars per cell per year; cells = area of the latitude band ÷ π (w/2)². Most cells are over
  ocean or empty land.
- Other continuous coverage: dollars per km² of the band per year. A scaled payload on revisit coverage has
  no defined swath, so no service metric is shown.

The cost optimum can differ from the mass optimum: payload costs more per kg than bus, so missions whose
payload grows fast with altitude (optical) are pulled lower by cost than by mass.
